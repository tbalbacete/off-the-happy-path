"They're going to send me an offer!" I was thrilled for her and proud, and I couldn't help wishing this day had waited a little longer. She's one of the most talented young engineers I've ever mentored, maybe the most, and she was about to move on to her next chapter.

## Watching them get good

When you mentor people, especially exceptional ones, you come to know that one day their growth will take them to new pastures. You hope that the steps you took alongside them, for the brief, fleeting moment your paths were aligned, are ones they will look back on fondly.

I remember it like it was yesterday. We had spent a day doing rapid-fire interviews, and now we had to boil the list down to the few people we were willing to take on as interns. For me, there was only one option. I remember telling my boss as soon as we finished, "If we don't get her, none of them will compare." To this day I'm still not sure why, but she picked us as her first choice, and the story began.

She rapidly grew as an engineer and went on to build her own software solutions, integrations, and processes, and she handled every curveball and roadblock with humility, grace, and a drive that is remarkable. Watching someone go from a first pull request with 20 comments and 15 trips back to the drawing board to being seen as an expert in their craft and a core part of the team has been a gift.

Inevitably, the day comes when a mentee stops needing you, and that moment is so quiet you could hear a pin drop. Someone who used to come to me excited, with new ideas and questions about how to build them, was now showing me the cool things she had already done without my help or advice. She may or may not also be catching things I missed at times. I was *not* ready for that moment, and in an odd way, I wish I could go back to those early days, even for one day.

## Then they leave

She knows her potential, and unsurprisingly, another company saw it too. I knew one day she would outgrow her role, and I always quietly (and sometimes not so quietly) hoped we would find something for her when that day came. We didn't. Oh, how common that is.

So many things are leaving with her. An infectious attitude and sense of humor. A shockingly deep understanding of our business, built in such a short time. A person who truly wants to leave things better than she found them, another rare quality. None of these things show up in the exit paperwork. Most of all, the way she brightened every one of our days leaves with her too, and that can't be replaced.

## Why AI makes this worse, not better

It's tempting. Hire more seniors, because AI can do the work juniors do. We can't get the budget to promote or keep this person right now, so let's wait. It sounds reasonable, right up until that exceptionally talented person sees your company in the rearview mirror, and you're left hoping that one day they'll find their way back. AI can make us faster, but it can't replace human relationships, curiosity, and judgment.

The work that's left once AI gets involved makes the need to grow and keep talent even clearer. Intuition, spotting the wrong answer that looks right, owning systems and processes, building rapport with the business: none of those can be handed to AI. I've seen it countless times. The people who grow into those skills most easily are the young, motivated people we now either take for granted or treat as disposable.

If we don't hire and grow our juniors now, who will be the seniors in five or ten years? Who will pick up the plates that rapid AI adoption is inevitably going to break?

## Hire them, grow them, keep them

### Hire for drive

When we picked her, it wasn't the résumé that stood out. It was the way she thought out loud, the questions she asked back, and how much she wanted to get it right. You can teach someone a framework in a few weeks. You can't teach them to care.

A junior's résumé won't tell you much, and that's fine. Look for curiosity. Watch how they handle "I don't know," and whether they get more interested or less when a problem gets hard. Those are the people who will keep learning faster than the tools change.

### Coach on the calendar

"My door is always open" isn't coaching. Juniors don't want to interrupt, and the questions they don't ask are usually the ones that matter most. Put real time on the calendar and protect it. Treat code review as teaching: explain the why, not just the what, and give the twentieth comment the same patience as the first.

Then give them something real to own. Not the ticket nobody else wants, but a piece of the system people depend on. Nothing builds judgment like being the person who gets the call when it breaks.

### Keep them before the offer letter

This is where we usually fall short. Growth is easy to see in hindsight and easy to miss while it's happening. By the time someone asks for a promotion, they've often been doing the job for months. By the time they bring you an offer, they've usually already decided.

So notice it first. Promote and pay ahead of the market, not in response to it. Show them the next step here, with a title and a timeline, so they don't have to go looking for it somewhere else. It isn't only money, either: interesting problems, real ownership, and credit given out loud all keep people around. And if the budget isn't there, say so plainly and fight for it anyway, where they can see you doing it.

<mark>The cheapest senior engineer you will ever find is the junior you already have.</mark>

## Letting go well

Some people will leave anyway, no matter what you do, and that's okay. Sometimes it's the whole point. If you did your job well, you helped someone grow beyond what their current environment could provide. Being someone people are glad they worked with still counts for a lot.

There's a line near the end of *The Office* that I keep coming back to.[^finale]

<figure class="pull-quote">
  <blockquote>
    <p>I wish there was a way to know you’re in the good old days before you’ve actually left them.</p>
  </blockquote>
  <figcaption>Andy Bernard, <cite>The Office</cite></figcaption>
</figure>

Andy's right that there's no way to know for sure. But I think you can get close. If you're lucky enough to be working with someone like her, assume you're in them. Act like it while you still can: teach them everything you know, put the time on the calendar, and fight for the promotion before somebody else offers it.

Those early days, with the 20-comment pull requests and the questions that slowly turned into ideas I hadn't thought of, were the good old days. I'm grateful I got to be part of them.

So hire the curious, driven ones. Grow them. Fight to keep them. And when it's time, be the one cheering loudest on their way out the door.

To the one who's leaving: thank you. Go make the next place better than you found it, just like you did here.
