"They're going to send me an offer!" The rush of bittersweet feelings that followed was something I never could have expected. Despite feeling so excited for and proud of her, I couldn't help but wish this day waited a little bit longer to come. One of, if not the most, talented young individuals I have ever had the chance to work with and mentor, received a job offer to leave the company at which we both work.

## Watching them get good

<!-- Who they were when they started: the first PR, the questions, the first time they caught something you missed. Make it specific so readers think of their own person. -->
When you mentor people, especially exceptional ones, you come to know that one day their growth journey will take them to new pastures. You hope that they look at the steps you took alongside them for that brief, fleeting moment that your paths were aligned, are ones that they will look back on fondly.

I remember the feeling like it was yesterday. Having to boil down a list of people whom we spent a day doing rapid-fire speed interviews with, to a small list of people we would be willing to take on as interns. For me, there was only one option. I remember immediately telling my boss after we finished, "if we don't get her, none of them will compare". To this day I am still not sure why, but she picked us as her #1 option, and the story began.

From seeing her grow as an engineer, to developing her own software solutions, she handled every curve ball and road block with humility, grace, and a consistent drive that is unique beyond measure. Seeing someone go from that first pull request with 20 comments and 15 returns back to the drawing board, to being looked at as in expert in their craft has been remarkable.

<!-- The point where they stopped needing you, and how quiet that moment is. -->
Inevitibly, the day comes where they stop needing you...and that moment is so quiet you could hear a pin drop. Someone who came to me excited and ready with new ideas asking how to implement them, now was showing me the cool things they already did without needing my help or advice. She may or may not also be finding things that I didn't even see now. I was NOT ready for that moment, and in an odd way, I wish I could go back for one day to those early days. 

## Then they leave

<!-- Why they usually go: a bigger title, more money, a manager who saw them sooner, or nobody here noticed they'd outgrown the role. Be honest about which of those were in your control. -->
She knows her potential, and fortunately for her, another company saw it too. I knew one day she'd outgrow her role, and I always quietly (and sometimes not so quietly) hoped we would find something for her when that day came. We didn't. Oh how common that is.

<!-- What leaves with them: context, judgment, the person juniors went to for help. None of it shows up in the exit paperwork. -->
So many things are leaving with her. An infectious attitude and sense of humor. A shockingly deep understanding of our business in such a short period of time. A person who truly wants to leave things better than she found them, another rare quality. None of these things show up in exit paperwork. Most importantly, the way she brightened all of our days every day also leaves with her. Something that can't be replaced.

## Why AI makes this worse, not better

<!-- The tempting story: AI writes the code, so we need fewer juniors. Push back on it. -->
It's tempting. Hire more seniors, AI can do the work juniors do. We can't get budget to retain or promote this person now, so let's wait. It sound tempting and reasonable, until that expectional talented individual sees your company in the review mirror while you wish you that one day they'd see their way back. AI can make us faster, it cannot replace human relationships, curiosity, and judgment. 

<!-- The work that's left is judgment: knowing what to build, spotting the wrong answer that looks right, owning the system. You only get that by doing the work for years with someone guiding you. -->
The work that is left after AI gets involved even more expemplifies the need for growing and retaining talent. Intuition, knowing wrong things that look right, owning systems and processes, building rapport with the business, are all things you can't replace with AI. I've seen it countless times; the people that most easily build into expertise in these skills are the young and motivated talent that we now either take for granted or see as disposable.

<!-- If nobody hires and grows juniors now, where do the seniors come from in five or ten years? -->
If we don't hire and grow our juniors now, who will be the seniors in five or ten years? Who will pick up some of the inevitable broken plates of the rapid AI adoption we have seen?
